Small amounts that leave your account every month are easy to overlook. Together they can add up. This guide shows how to put them in one list in four steps and decide for each one whether it stays. It is written for people living in the Netherlands, so it uses Dutch rules where they matter.

## Step 1: find the recurring debits

Start with your bank statements. Take at least twelve months, otherwise you miss yearly charges such as an insurance, a domain name or an annual membership.

Look for:

- amounts that come back every week, month, quarter or year;
- the same description or the same creditor, even when the amount differs slightly;
- charges on a credit card or on a second account. If you bank with more than one provider, check all of them.

Free trials that turned into paid plans belong on the list too. You can spot them as a small amount that suddenly appears.

## Step 2: put them in a list

For each subscription, write down:

- the provider;
- the amount and how often it is charged;
- the amount per year, so you can compare them fairly;
- the date you signed up or the contract was renewed;
- how to cancel and the notice period.

A spreadsheet is enough. Convert everything to a yearly amount: €9.99 a month is almost €120 a year, which feels different from the monthly figure.

## Step 3: decide for each item

Ask three questions per line:

1. Did I use it in the past month?
2. Would I sign up today at this price?
3. Am I paying twice for the same thing, for example two streaming services or a plan that is already part of another bundle?

Then choose per line: keep, switch to a cheaper tier with the same provider, or cancel. That is your call; what is useless to one person is worth the money to another.

## Step 4: cancel and check

### What the Dutch consumer authority says

According to the ACM (accessed 26 October 2026), these rules apply, among others, to subscriptions and supply contracts sold to consumers in the Netherlands:

- The fixed term is at most 1 year. For telecom subscriptions, up to 2 years is allowed.
- After the fixed term, consumers can always cancel with a notice period of at most 1 month. For newspapers and magazines that appear less than once a month, the notice period may be 3 months.
- After the fixed term, the provider may renew the contract automatically. Newspapers and magazines may be renewed automatically for at most 3 months.
- Consumers can at least cancel in writing, for example by letter, web form or email. Cancelling the same way you signed up, such as by phone, is also allowed.

If your terms say something different, or you are unsure about your contract, read the ACM page itself (linked below) and the provider's terms. The ACM page is in Dutch.

### Cancelling in practice

- Cancel in writing and keep the confirmation.
- Note the end date the provider gives you and put it in your calendar.
- Check your next two statements to see that the charge really stops.
- If you pay by direct debit you can also ask your bank to block it, but still cancel with the provider: the contract does not end by itself.

## One option: automatic detection

You can do all of this by hand, in a spreadsheet or on paper. Some apps detect recurring charges automatically. LaVega does too: it looks for subscriptions in your transactions once you have imported bank exports or linked an account read-only. You do not need any tool for the four steps above.

## Make it a habit

Set aside fifteen minutes twice a year to go through the list again. Price increases and trial plans creep back in otherwise. How much a cleaned-up list frees up depends entirely on your own spending. For what a personal CFO is, see [What is a personal CFO?](/en/questions/what-is-a-personal-cfo).
